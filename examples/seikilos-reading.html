Word by word

ὅσον — as long as. A time expression introducing the clause ‘as long as you live’.

ζῇς — you live. From ζάω, to live: present indicative, second person singular. Compare the infinitive ζῆν in verse 3.

φαίνου — shine; show yourself. An imperative addressed to one person. Its first syllable, φαί, carries three rising pitches.

μηδὲν — not at all. Literally ‘nothing’, used here with the prohibition. The negative belongs with λυποῦ, ‘grieve’.

ὅλως — at all; entirely. An adverb strengthening the negative expression. The syllable λως carries two pitches.

σὺ — you. The singular pronoun explicitly addresses the listener. Greek can leave this subject implicit, so its presence is emphatic.

λυποῦ — grieve; be distressed. A present middle/passive imperative from λυπέω, used here with a negative. Two falling pitches share ποῦ, the syllable with a circumflex.

πρὸς — for. With ὀλίγον it forms a time expression: ‘for a little while’.

ὀλίγον — a little. Here, a short amount of time. The word crosses a physical line on the stone between λί and γον.

ἔστι — is; exists. From εἰμί, to be. The subject is τὸ ζῆν, ‘living’. The accented reading is editorial; the stone has capital letters.

τὸ — the. The neuter singular article turns the infinitive ζῆν, ‘to live’, into the noun-like expression ‘living’.

ζῆν — to live; living. The infinitive of ζάω. With τὸ it names the activity of living. Its circumflex syllable has a falling pair of pitches.

τὸ — the. Here the neuter article belongs to τέλος, the object of ‘demands’.

τέλος — end; completion. The object is placed before the subject: ‘the end, time demands’. English normally reverses that order.

ὁ — the. The masculine singular article belongs to χρόνος, the subject of the final clause.

χρόνος — time. The subject of ἀπαιτεῖ. Follow the word across two physical lines: χρό at the end of one, νος at the start of the next.

ἀπαιτεῖ — demands; claims as due. From ἀπαιτέω, present indicative, third person singular. The last syllable has three falling pitches. Its final sign is a reversed gamma, not a square bracket.

Greek is normalized with accents, breathing marks, word divisions and four verse lines. The photograph preserves six physical song lines. Roman letters are a reading aid, not recorded pronunciation. English and notes are new teaching translations; no Latin translation survives on this object.
