# Six times, different genera

This room reads Aristoxenus, _Rhythmic Elements_ II, in Rudolf Westphal,
_Die Fragmente und die Lehrsätze der griechischen Rhythmiker_ (Leipzig:
B. G. Teubner, 1861), printed pp.28, 31, 33–37. It concerns rhythmic
organization, rather than recovering a melody or an instrument's sound.

## What a learner can do

Keep a foot at six primary times. Change its two portions from 3+3 to 2+4.
The total stays six, while the genus changes from the equal/dactylic to the
double/iambic. Reverse the up and down portions: their order changes while
the total and genus remain. Join the internal attacks without moving the
foot's defining division. Then inspect a different example in which the down
portion stays two times and the up portion lies between one and two.

A reference and chosen foot share the same total primary-time count and modern
unit duration. The reference uses the integer division nearest the middle.
It is a modern comparison choice; a seven-time reference, for example, falls
outside the three admitted genera too. Every reference is labelled by its own
computed ratio and status, rather than silently treated as an ancient norm.

## The distinctions in the fragment

- A primary time is not divided by the material being rhythmized (p.31). The
  browser's 180–600 ms unit is a chosen modern duration, not an ancient tempo.
- Foot magnitude and genus are distinct differences (p.35). Genus concerns the
  ratio of the portions. It is not fixed by the total number of units alone.
- The three genera admitted for continuous rhythmic composition here are the
  equal/dactylic, double/iambic and sesquialter/paeonic (p.36). These names do
  not require every foot to have one stereotyped poetic syllable pattern.
- Six admits both equal and double divisions (p.37). The 5:1 division is
  rejected in this passage. Seven has no division into these admitted ratios.
  The eight-time statement breaks off after 'because'; its missing explanation
  is not supplied as recovered text.
- Foot-preserving markers and subdivisions made by rhythmic composition are
  explicitly distinguished (pp.33–34). Unit clicks or joined portions in this
  room are modern illustrations of that distinction.
- Antithesis concerns equal feet whose up/down assignments oppose one another
  (p.36). The reverse command exchanges the assigned durations; it has an
  effect when the portions are unequal.

Our exact integer classifier is deliberately narrow: it reports membership in
the three stated ratio families, with primary-time counts 3 through 8. It is
not a validator for all Greek music or for rhythm in general. Westphal prints
later parallels from Psellus and other authors on the same pages. In particular,
Psellus permits additional ratios in a separate passage. Those witnesses are
not silently merged into Aristoxenus's surviving fragment.

## The intermediate example and numerical rationality

On p.34, the text compares two feet with a down portion of two times. One has
an up portion of two, the other of one. A third has its up portion intermediate
between those; it is called a _choreios alogos_. The source does not prescribe
an exact numerical midpoint. Our 1.5 is an explicit illustrative choice.

The resulting up:down ratio is 1.5:2 = 3:4, a rational fraction in ordinary
arithmetic. The next passage distinguishes numerical ratios from rhythmic
recognizability, and p.35 qualifies a common measure as itself _enrhythmon_,
rhythmically admissible. Merely rescaling a numerical grid does not establish
that musical condition. We do not classify every intervening ratio, set a
modern perceptual threshold or claim to have tested a listener's recognition.
The three examples fix a 300 ms unit; their overall durations differ because
the down portion stays fixed while the up portion changes.

## Representation and sound

Up and down are the source's _anō/katō_, _arsis/basis_. The bars are a modern
temporal diagram, not a reconstructed bodily movement or an assertion that
ancient arsis/thesis correspond exactly to modern weak/strong accents. The
primary bench lays up before down for comparison; the intermediate example
lays the fixed down portion first. The text's duration argument does not
require those display orientations.

Sine clicks provide modern markers. Up, down and optional inner attacks have
chosen different frequencies to make them distinguishable. They are not
ancient absolute pitches, a recovered percussion timbre or a historical accent
pattern. Both playback and WAV use Pyknon's existing Web Audio graph at its
48 kHz offline rendering rate, with finite envelopes. A unit is at least
180 ms and each click lasts 120 ms, leaving time for the shared attack/release.
The comparison repeats each foot twice, with an explicit gap between them.
Playback highlighting follows the audio clock; control changes, page departure
and hidden tabs stop prior playback.

## Text and saved sources

Greek passages are transcribed from the main Aristoxenus text above Westphal's
apparatus rules. Ligatures, spacing, line breaks and punctuation are normalized;
footnote calls are omitted. New English and contextual word notes accompany
them. The full pages retain variant readings and parallel witnesses. A short
Latin parallel is identified as Westphal's citation 'Mar. Victor. p.2485', not
as a Latin translation of Aristoxenus or a resolved authorship attribution.

The source manifest gives the public-domain 1861 scan, original PDF identity,
physical/printed page mapping and each bundled image hash. Saved HTML verifies
source images and Greek font before embedding them, includes the selected
comparison and intermediate example, both timing tables, readings, word notes,
editable JSON and chosen synthetic listening WAV, and executes no scripts.
